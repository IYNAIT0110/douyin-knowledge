# Part B · 论证展开（整段英文 → 整段中文）
# Part B · The Argument in Full (English Paragraph First)

## B1. "Cleaning" Understates What the Work Actually Is

**EN**
Calling it data cleaning undersells it badly, and that is why so many teams skip it. Cleaning sounds like removing mojibake and deduplicating rows — necessary, boring, and impossible to differentiate on. But look at what the pipeline is actually deciding, and it turns out to be a chain of product judgements, not a janitorial task. It decides which ten chunks come back for a query, which decides whether your retrieval beats a generic web search. It decides whether a sentence is a fact or somebody's view, which decides whether that sentence goes straight into a conclusion or into a pile that needs cross-checking. It decides whether an event is critical or trivial, which decides whether anything downstream bothers to fire at all. Each one is a judgement call, and the quality of a judgement call is a function of how long you have been making it. That is the actual moat: not that competitors cannot do these things, but that they would be paying for them again on every call, while you already paid once, up front.

> **中**
> 把这件事叫"数据清洗"严重低估了它，而这也正是很多团队跳过它的原因。清洗听起来就是去掉乱码、去重 —— 必要、无聊，而且没法拿来做出差异。但你去看这条管线真正在决定什么，就会发现它是一连串产品判断，不是保洁工作。它决定一次查询召回哪十个块，而这决定了你的检索能不能打赢通用网络搜索。它决定一句话是事实还是某人的看法，而这决定了这句话是直接进结论，还是进一堆需要交叉验证的东西。它决定一个事件是 critical 还是无关紧要，而这决定了下游到底要不要启动。每一个都是判断，而判断的质量取决于你做了多久。这才是真正的护城河：不是竞争对手做不到这些事，而是他们要在每一次调用上重新付一遍钱，而你已经一次性付完了。

## B2. Why the Fact/Opinion Split Has to Happen Upstream

**EN**
It is tempting to leave the fact-versus-opinion question to the model at inference time, because modern models are decent at it and it saves you building the pipeline. That trade looks fine on a demo and gets expensive in production, for two reasons. The first is cost: a judgement you make once when ingesting a document is paid for once, while a judgement made at query time is paid for on every query, forever, by every user. The second is stability: if the classification happens live, it can come out differently on Monday and on Tuesday, and any downstream logic that assumed a stable split now has to tolerate that wobble. There is also a subtle third cost that is easy to miss. When facts and opinions sit in the same field, your retrieval treats them as interchangeable, so a query for a number can return somebody's view of that number, and the model will report it with the same confidence. Separating them is not a tidiness preference; it is what makes the two downstream paths possible at all.

> **中**
> 把"事实还是观点"这个问题留给模型在推理时判断，是很诱人的 —— 现代模型在这件事上做得还行，而且你能省掉搭管线的功夫。这笔买卖在 demo 上看着不错，上了生产就很贵，原因有两个。第一个是成本：你在摄入文档时做一次判断，就只付一次钱；而在查询时做的判断，每一次查询、每一个用户、永远都要付。第二个是稳定性：如果分类是实时发生的，它周一和周二可能给出不同结果，而任何假定这个分流是稳定的下游逻辑，现在都得容忍这种抖动。还有第三个容易漏掉的隐性成本：当事实和观点躺在同一个字段里，你的检索会把它们当成可以互换的东西，于是查一个数值的 query 可能召回到某人对这个数值的看法，而模型会用同样的自信把它报出来。把它们分开不是洁癖，它是那两条下游路径能够存在的前提。

## B3. Efficiency Debates Usually Collapse Two Different Time Scales

**EN**
The argument about whether inference efficiency matters is mostly a category error, and once you name the variable the argument dissolves. The variable is not efficiency — it is the time scale of the task, and more specifically whether a human is waiting. If a person is sitting in front of the output, latency is the product: ten minutes before a meeting, a deck that arrives in ninety seconds rather than four minutes is a different product, not a faster version of the same one. If nobody is waiting — if the agent runs overnight and you read the result at breakfast — then latency stops being the product and collapses back into cost, which is a much milder claim. Notice that the mild version is still true and still worth money: cutting a day to half a day saves compute even when nobody is watching. So the rule is not "efficiency matters" or "efficiency does not matter". The rule is to ask whether anyone is waiting, and then how much the wait is costing you. Most efficiency debates are two people answering that question about two different tasks.

> **中**
> "推理效率到底重不重要"这场争论，基本上是一个范畴错误 —— 一旦你把那个变量点名，争论就散了。那个变量不是效率，而是任务的时间尺度，更具体地说：有没有人在等。如果有人正坐在输出前面，延迟就是产品本身：开会前十分钟，一份九十秒到而不是四分钟到的 deck，是另一件产品，而不是同一件产品的更快版本。如果没有人在等 —— 智能体跑一整夜，你早饭时看结果 —— 那延迟就不再是产品，它塌缩回成本，这是一个温和得多的论断。注意这个温和版本依然成立、也依然值钱：把一天压成半天，就算没人盯着，也省了算力。所以规则既不是"效率重要"，也不是"效率不重要"。规则是：先问有没有人在等，再问这份等待值多少钱。大多数关于效率的争论，都是两个人在拿两个不同尺度任务回答同一个问题。

# Part C · Extras (English Only — Chinese Folded for Self-Check)

## C1. Three Questions to Ask Before You Optimise Anything

*Terms: **blast radius** = how much an event or change affects; **latency** = delay before you get the result; **collapse into** = reduce to something simpler.*

Here is the reusable version, in three questions, and it works on far more than inference speed. First: what is the time scale of this task — minutes, hours, or days? Second: is anyone actually waiting for the output, and would they notice the difference? Third: when you improve it, are you saving money or saving somebody's waiting? Answer those in order and the priority usually settles itself. Saving waiting is worth a lot when a human is present and worth almost nothing when nobody checks until morning. Saving money is always worth something, but it is a steady, unglamorous return, and it rarely justifies the heroic effort people pour into latency. The reason this is worth writing down is that most engineering arguments about speed are actually two people holding different answers to question one while shouting about question three.

## C2. The Moat Is Prepaid Cost, Not Secret Knowledge

*Terms: **prepay** = pay once in advance; **on every call** = each time the system runs; **undifferentiated** = not distinguishable from competitors'.*

One more reframe, because it changes how you would audit a company — or your own project. A moat built on dirty work is not built on secrets. Nothing in the pipeline is unknowable; a competitor could in principle build every stage of it. What they cannot do is build it for free or build it quickly, because the asset is not the design but the accumulated prepayment: cleaning done, categories settled, grades assigned, clusters formed, edge cases already argued through and encoded. Every query that arrives at a system like this is cheaper to serve than the same query arriving at a system that has to improvise. So when you want to know whether a data project is actually defensible, do not ask whether the approach is clever. Ask a duller question: which costs has this system already paid that a new entrant would have to pay again on every single run? If the answer is "none", the cleverness is borrowed, and borrowed cleverness expires with the next model release.
