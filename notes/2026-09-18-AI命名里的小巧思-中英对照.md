# Part B · 论证展开（整段英文 → 整段中文）
# Part B · The Argument in Full (English Paragraph First)

## B1. Naming Is Cognitive Anchoring

**EN**
A brand-new word carries nothing. Say a string of letters and digits to someone and they have nowhere to put it, so it slides off the next morning. This is why every lab reaches backwards instead of forwards. Latin anchors to universality and authority, poetry anchors to craft and completeness, game theory anchors to rigour and proof, science fiction anchors to the future and to otherness. Each borrowing is a decision about which old structure the new thing should be filed under in the listener's head. The consequence is uncomfortable in a useful way: **the speed at which you accept a name is the speed at which you accept the technology**. A model called Magnum Opus arrives pre-loaded with an expectation of greatness, and that expectation does real work on your judgement before you have run a single test. Which is also why the "AI is like a nuclear weapon" comparison spread so fast — it is the same mechanism, borrowing an old concept to anchor a new one, aimed in a direction almost everyone in the field thinks is wrong.

> **中**
> 一个全新的词不携带任何东西。你对别人念一串字母和数字，他脑子里没有地方放它，第二天早上就滑走了。这就是为什么每一家实验室都往后取、而不是往前造。翻拉丁文锚定的是普世与权威，翻诗集锚定的是作品感与完成度，翻博弈论锚定的是严谨与可证明，翻科幻锚定的是未来与异质。每一次借用，都是在决定"这个新东西应该被归到听众脑子里哪一个旧结构下面"。由此得出的结论让人不太舒服但很有用：**你接受一个名字的速度，就是你接受这个技术的速度。** 一个叫 Magnum Opus 的东西，在被你实测之前就已经预装了一份"它应该很伟大"的期待，而这份期待会实实在在地干扰你的判断。同样的机制也解释了"AI 像核武器"这个类比为什么传得这么快 —— 它也是借旧概念去锚定新东西，只不过锚反了方向。

## B2. When the Chinese Name and the English Name Disagree

**EN**
The most informative cases are the mismatches, and Kimi is the cleanest one in this video. In Chinese the company is 月之暗面, the dark side of the moon, taken from a Pink Floyd album — literary, almost wistful. In English the same company is Moonshot AI, a word borrowed from the Apollo programme that means a project so ambitious that landing it changes the world. Two names, two ambitions, two audiences. The mismatch tells you something a matched pair would hide: names are rarely the output of one unified branding exercise. They get made at different moments by different people under different pressures, and each one leaks whatever that person was defending at the time. So when you see a company with a poetic Chinese name and a hard-edged English one, do not assume sloppiness. Read it as two statements made in two rooms, and ask what each room needed to be true.

> **中**
> 信息量最大的是那些不一致的情况，而视频里最干净的例子就是 Kimi。中文叫"月之暗面"，来自 Pink Floyd 的专辑《The Dark Side of the Moon》，很文艺，甚至有点怅然；同一家公司的英文名却叫 Moonshot，这个词从登月计划里引申而来，指那种野心极大、一旦做成就能改变世界的项目。两个名字，两种野心，两拨听众。这种不一致泄露了一件"一致"会藏起来的事：名字很少是一次统一品牌策划的产物。它们是在不同时间、由不同的人、顶着不同的压力拍出来的，每一个都泄露了当时那个人想要捍卫的东西。所以看到一家公司中文名很诗意、英文名很硬的时候，别急着判定为不专业。把它当成两个房间里说出的两句话，然后问一句：每个房间各自需要什么成立。

## B3. Grok Is the Most Honest Name in the Batch

**EN**
Most of these names are about scale: bigger, broader, grander. Grok is the one that is about a relationship, and it is worth pulling out for that reason. It comes from a science-fiction novel, where it is Martian for "drink", and by extension for understanding something so completely that you merge with it. Engineers are said to use it internally — "I grok it" — to mean they have not just parsed the code but absorbed it. That is a precise description of what an assistant is actually for. The useful version of an assistant is not a thing that knows the answer; it is a thing that has taken in your context so thoroughly that it no longer needs the context restated. Every other name in this video describes how big the model is. Grok describes how close it should get.

> **中**
> 这批名字里大多数讲的是规模：更大、更广、更宏大。Grok 是唯一一个讲关系的名字，所以值得单独拎出来。它出自一本科幻小说，最初是火星语里"喝"的意思，引申为彻底理解，甚至跟理解的对象融为一体。据说内部程序员会说 "I grok it"，意思不是"我看懂了"，而是"我吃透了"。这恰恰是对"助手到底该干什么"最精确的描述。一个助手的有用形态，不是那个知道答案的东西，而是那个把你的语境吃得足够透、以至于你不必再复述一遍的东西。视频里其余的名字都在描述模型有多大，只有 Grok 在描述它应该离你多近。

# Part C · 延伸（纯英文，中文折叠自查）
# Part C · Extras (English Only — Chinese Folded for Self-Check)

## C1. A Three-Step Way to Read Any Product Name

*Terms: **anchor** = the old idea a new thing is attached to; **coin a word** = invent a brand-new word; **leak** = reveal unintentionally.*

Here is the reusable part, stated once. The next time a lab announces something with an unfamiliar name, run three questions before you read anything else. First, where was it borrowed from — Latin, literature, mathematics, scripture, science fiction? The shelf tells you the register the team wants. Second, what does the borrowed word mean literally, and is there an ordering hidden in it? Claude's three names are not three labels; they are a ladder, and the ladder tells you the product line-up without a benchmark. Third, is there a mismatch between the name in one language and the name in another, and what does each version need to be true? Three questions, roughly two minutes, and you will have read the announcement more carefully than most people who watched the whole keynote.

## C2. Names Age Badly, and That Is the Interesting Part

*Terms: **outgrow** = grow past; **on its merits** = judged by actual performance, not reputation.*

There is a second reason to read names carefully: they are the most dated part of any product. A model that ships as Haiku this year will be doing Haiku's job with next year's weights, and the name will have stopped describing anything. That decay is measurable and it is worth watching, because it tells you when a label has become pure branding. The moment a name stops predicting anything about the thing it labels, it has moved from description into advertising, and from that point on it only does the dangerous half of the job — it primes your expectation without informing it. The discipline is simple and slightly unfair: use the name to orient yourself once, then judge the thing on its merits and stop letting the name do any further work. Names are a good way in. They are a bad place to stop.

## 落地清单（中文速览 / 无英文）

按"先做什么最划算"排序：

1. **记住 Claude 那条阶梯** —— 俳句 < 十四行诗 < 毕生杰作，比记参数表轻松；看到 Fable → Mythos 的跳跃，顺带知道产品在往"成体系"走
