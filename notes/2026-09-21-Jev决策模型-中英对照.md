# Part B · The Argument in Detail (Paragraph by Paragraph)

## B1. The Problem Nobody Was Measuring

**EN**
Every AI pipeline I have seen has the same quiet bottleneck: we call a generative LLM to answer a question whose answer is a label. A support ticket arrives and we need to know which queue it belongs in. An email lands and we need to know whether it is phishing. A prompt hits the API and we need to know whether someone is trying to jailbreak it. For each of those we pay 500 milliseconds to two seconds of streaming tokens, real inference spend, and then a parser we have to maintain, because the model replied in prose. And the confidence score we asked for is theatre: the model predicted tokens that sound confident, nothing more. Bootstrap automation on top of that and you have built a control system on top of a number with no mathematical grounding. That is the gap Jev walks into, and it is a much more boring gap than "models are getting smarter".

> **中**
> 我见过的每条 AI 流水线都有同一个安静的瓶颈：我们调用生成式 LLM，去回答一个答案其实是标签的问题。来了一张工单，我们想知道它归哪个队列；来了一封邮件，我们想知道它是不是钓鱼；一个 prompt 打进 API，我们想知道对方是不是在做越狱。每一个这类场景，我们都要付 500 毫秒到 2 秒的流式生成时间、真金白银的推理成本，外加一个需要自己维护的解析器 —— 因为模型是用自然语言回答的。而我们顺手要来的 confidence 分数更是表演性质：模型只是预测了"听起来很有把握的 token"，仅此而已。在这种东西上面搭自动化，等于把一个控制系统建在一个没有数学基础的数上。这就是 Jev 走进来的那个缺口，而且它比"模型越来越聪明"无聊得多。

## B2. Assistance Is Not Automation

**EN**
Diogo made this argument in a talk roughly a month before launch, and in hindsight the talk reads as the product's design document. Start from the contradiction: models can now attack hard mathematics and climb every benchmark, yet in the places companies actually want automation, the human never gets removed. Customer support is his example. Let the AI look things up, summarise documents, draft a reply — fine. But let the AI decide whether the refund is approved, and everyone gets cautious. Why is the easy one harder? Because of how these models were trained. RLHF collects human preferences and pushes the model toward what humans like, which means, quite literally, we put the human into the loop during training and then wondered why we cannot take the human out at inference time. He has a story for it: someone sent ChatGPT a recording of themselves farting, called it an original piece of music, and asked for sincere feedback — and got a warm, detailed appreciation of its eerie ambient qualities.

> **中**
> Diogo 在发布前一个多月的一场演讲里讲了这个论点，事后回看，那场演讲几乎就是这款产品的设计文档。他从一个矛盾出发：模型今天已经能挑战高难度数学、在各类 benchmark 上一路向上，但在企业真正想做自动化的地方，人始终没被拿掉。客服就是他的例子。让 AI 查资料、总结文档、起草回复 —— 没问题。但让 AI 决定这笔钱到底退不退，所有人立刻变得谨慎。为什么简单的事反而难？因为这些模型的训练方式。RLHF 收集人类偏好，然后把模型推向人喜欢的方向 —— 也就是说，我们在训练时字面意义上把人塞进了回路里，事后却疑惑为什么推理时把人拿不出来。他有个现成的段子：有人给 ChatGPT 发了一段自己放屁的录音，说这是原创音乐，请它真诚评价，结果得到了一段温情而详尽的赏析，称赞它带有阴森的环境音乐质感。

## B3. Two Routes, and What the Demo Showed

**EN**
It helps to name the two routes explicitly, because the video spends its first minute setting them side by side. The mainstream route pushes on capability: stronger intelligence, deeper reasoning, agents that can take a complicated task and finish it. Jev takes the opposite bet and picks one very small point. The line that carries the whole design is almost boring when you read it alone: when you are making a judgment, you do not actually need to wait that long or burn that many tokens — give a rough call, give the probabilities attached to the different outcomes, and you are done. Everyone else is making the output longer; this deletes the output. The demo makes the shape of that difference impossible to unsee. On the same structured output, the decision model produces the whole thing in one piece, while the autoregressive model is still spelling itself out token by token, and only when it finishes can anything downstream act. That is not a speed gap that better engineering closes, and the video is blunt about why: the limitation belongs to the Transformer architecture itself, and that limitation is what forces the wait. Which is another way of saying the latency is not a bug someone will optimise away next quarter — it is the price of the shape.

> **中**
> 把两条路线明确点名是有用的，因为视频第一分钟就在把它们并排放。主流路线押的是能力：更强的智能、更深的推理、能接下复杂任务并干完的 Agent。Jev 押的是反面，只挑了一个非常小的点。承载整套设计的那句话单独看几乎平淡无奇：你在做判断的时候，其实并不需要等那么久、烧那么多 token —— 给个大概的判断，给个不同结果各自的概率，就够了。别人都在把输出变长；它把输出删掉。现场演示让这个差别的**形状**变得无法忽视。同样是结构化输出，决策模型是整块一次性出来，而自回归模型还在逐 token 往外拼，只有拼完了下游才能动。这不是靠更好的工程能补上的速度差，视频对原因说得很直白：**局限属于 Transformer 这个架构本身**，正是这个局限逼着我们等。换句话说，延迟不是下个季度会被优化掉的 bug —— 它是这个形状的价格。

## B4. What Calibration Actually Buys You

**EN**
The interesting part of RLCD is not that it makes the model smarter. It makes the model's own numbers usable. A strictly proper scoring rule — the Brier score is the usual one — punishes miscalibration: if you say 80%, you had better be right about 80% of the time, and no clever hedging gets you out of it. Once that holds, three things open up at once. First, thresholds become meaningful: above the line, act; below it, escalate. Second, cost planning becomes possible, because you can predict how often the expensive fallback gets triggered. Third, auditing becomes possible, because you can look back at a month of decisions and check whether the 90% bucket actually resolved 90% of the time. None of that is available with today's "sounds confident" numbers. The video expresses the same idea in plainer language: the real problem is not that the AI doesn't know — it's that the AI doesn't know that it doesn't know.

> **中**
> RLCD 有意思的地方不在于让模型更聪明，而在于让模型自己输出的数字变得**可用**。严格适当评分规则（通常用 Brier score）会惩罚校准失准：你说 80%，那就最好真的在 80% 的情况下应验，任何机灵的圆滑都救不了你。一旦这一点成立，三件事同时被打开。第一，阈值变得有意义：线以上直接执行，线以下升级处理。第二，成本变得可预估，因为你能算出昂贵兜底方案会被触发的频率。第三，审计成为可能：你可以回头看一个月的所有决策，验证"90% 那一档"是不是真的有 90% 应验。这三件事在今天的"听起来很有把握"式数字上全都做不到。视频用的是更朴素的说法：真正麻烦的不是 AI 不知道，而是 AI 不知道自己不知道。

## B5. Where the Decisions Will Live

**EN**
The largest jump in the video arrives near the end, and it is not about models at all. Up to this point the pitch has been substitution: replace one LLM call with a cheaper, faster decision. Then the frame widens, and the claim becomes that every piece of software may end up running a decision model behind it. Notice what it is compared against — not a chatbot, but the classifier. The old way is the one anyone who has shipped a feature knows: gather labelled data, train a classifier, spend half a day tuning it, then maintain it forever as the world drifts. The new way, in the video's own phrasing, is that we used to call that training a classifier and now we simply do not need to. You describe the situation and the outcomes you care about, and the judgement comes back. Once judgement costs roughly nothing, AI stops being a destination you visit on purpose and becomes a default step baked into the software, which is a different order of magnitude entirely: not you calling AI at one point in the flow, but every screen, every click and every log line quietly carrying a judgement you never asked for and never see. And the first candidates for replacement are not the glamorous ones. They are the ugly artifacts nobody files under AI at all — regexes, keyword blocklists, if-else rule trees, rule engines kept alive by hand. They never appeared in anyone's estimate of what AI could do, which is exactly why they are both the first to go and the easiest to overlook.

> **中**
> 视频里最大的一次跳跃出现在临近结尾处，而且它讲的根本不是模型。到这一步为止，卖点一直是**替换**：把某次 LLM 调用换成一个更便宜、更快的判断。然后画框被拉宽，主张变成了——每一个软件背后，可能都会跑着一套决策模型。注意它拿来对比的是什么：不是聊天机器人，而是**分类器**。老办法是任何一个上线过功能的人都熟悉的：攒标注数据、训一个分类器、调上半天，然后在世界慢慢漂移的过程中永久维护它。新办法，用视频自己的措辞，就是我们以前管那叫"训练一个分类器"，而现在**不需要了**。你描述情境、说清你在意哪些结果，判断就回来了。一旦判断的成本约等于零，AI 就不再是你专程去拜访的一个去处，而是被烧进软件的一道默认工序 —— 这是完全不同的量级：不是你在流程的某一步调用了 AI，而是每一个界面、每一次点击、每一行日志都悄悄带着一次你没要求过、也看不见的判断。而最先被替换的候选并不光鲜。它们是那些根本没人归到 AI 名下的丑东西 —— 正则、关键词黑名单、if-else 规则树、靠手工续命的规则引擎。它们从来没出现在任何人对"AI 能做什么"的估算里，正因如此，它们既是最先被换掉的一批，也是最容易漏掉的一批。

## B6. Where This Goes Next

**EN**
Two directions are worth watching. The first is boring and commercial: any workflow where a human now reads something just to classify it is a candidate, and the list is long — routing, triage, moderation, compliance screening, you name it. The second is more interesting. Agents, once they actually run, produce an enormous number of micro-decisions: which tool to call, is this page element the right one to click, has this task actually finished, should this result be re-checked. Each one is easy; the aggregate is not. If every micro-decision costs two seconds and a stream of tokens, the economics of an autonomous agent never close. At Jev's price and latency they might, which is why tools like this get picked up as agent evaluators and context filters almost immediately. The counter-argument is also visible already: at least one independent researcher published the non-autoregressive decision idea a year earlier with papers and open weights, and got roughly no attention. Ideas don't win because they are first; they win when the surrounding conditions make them necessary.

> **中**
> 接下来有两条值得看的方向。第一条无聊但商业：任何"现在需要人读一眼只为做个分类"的环节都是候选，名单很长 —— 路由分发、工单分诊、内容审核、合规初筛，等等。第二条更有意思。Agent 一旦真的跑起来，会产生海量的微小判断：调用哪个工具、页面上该点哪个元素、这个任务到底完成了没有、这条结果要不要复核。单个都简单，聚合起来要命。如果每一次微判断都要两秒加一串 token，自治 Agent 的经济账永远算不平。而在 Jev 的价格和延迟下，账有可能平 —— 所以这类工具几乎立刻就被拿去当 Agent 评估器和上下文过滤器用了。反面材料也已经出现：至少有一位独立研究者在一年前就发了论文和开源权重做过同一件事，几乎没得到关注。**想法不是因为先到才赢，而是在外部条件终于需要它时才赢。**

## C3. The Counter-Claim, Worth Knowing

An independent researcher named Adil Sadqi has publicly pointed out that he built non-autoregressive decision models with reinforcement learning a year earlier, published two arXiv papers (arXiv:2503.23303 in March 2025 and arXiv:2510.01237 in September 2025), released open weights on Hugging Face, published his dataset, and shipped a Python package — without anybody noticing. He then rebuilt the idea on a bidirectional encoder and reports 33 to 38 milliseconds on a GPU, roughly four times faster than Jev's published latency, fully open source. The lesson is uncomfortable and useful: having the right idea early, with full openness, was not enough. What was missing was the moment — a market that had just started running millions of agent decisions a day and suddenly needed exactly this. There is a second lesson hiding in it for anyone keeping a learning log like this one: publishing your work is not the finish line, and being ignored is not the same as being wrong.

## C4. Matching Intelligence to the Job

The video closes on a bet the author says he has made before: the future probably belongs to small models. The claim is not that small models are smarter. It is that in each situation, intelligence matched to that situation is enough, and you do not need the most expensive intelligence sitting in every environment. He ties it to what he calls the token economy, and to a book he recently wrote on the same idea: use the most fitting means to reach the goal, and that is good enough. Put next to the pricing above, the argument stops being philosophical. If one route bills ten dollars per million tokens and another bills four cents and gives you a probability you can route on, then the interesting engineering question is no longer which model is strongest. It is which decisions in your system are worth paying the strong model for, and which ones were never decisions about intelligence at all — they were decisions about classification, and you have been paying generation prices for them.
